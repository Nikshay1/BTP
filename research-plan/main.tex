\section{Abstract and Problem Statement}

\subsection{Abstract}

Multi-agent systems built on large language models (LLMs) usually fix one way of working together before
the task is seen: a debate, a pipeline of roles, or a manager with workers. Real tasks are mixed. One part
needs a search over many candidate paths, another needs several independent opinions to be reconciled, and a
third is routine work that one worker can finish alone. When the same coordination pattern is applied to all
of them, the system pays for messages and tokens that do not improve the answer, and recent studies report
that such systems often fail to beat a well-prompted single agent. This project asks whether a coordinator
that picks a different coordination mechanism for each subtask can do better within the same budget. The
mechanisms are taken from animal behavior and written as ordinary algorithms: reinforced search (ant),
evidence accumulation with cross inhibition (bee), bounded local execution (octopus), tool-prerequisite
planning (crow) and checkpoint-based recovery (starfish), with two more kept as extensions. We will build
the framework, profile each mechanism on planning, tool-use and reasoning tasks whose answers can be checked
automatically, and learn a lightweight selector from the recorded outcomes. Adaptive allocation will be
compared with a single agent, the best single mechanism, fixed and random allocation, and a conventional
coordinator with specialist agents that is given the same model, tools and budget. The result will be a
working prototype and a measured account of when a mix of mechanisms helps and when it does not.

\subsection{Problem statement}

An LLM multi-agent system splits a task among agents that exchange messages. The split is useful only
if the way the agents coordinate suits the work. We expect a subtask with many possible solution paths to
benefit from exploration that remembers what worked, a subtask with a few competing answers to need those
answers compared and one of them chosen, and a well-specified subtask to need no discussion at all, since
involving the coordinator at every step only adds cost. These are hypotheses, and RQ1 tests them. Current
systems mostly choose one pattern for the whole task. The evidence reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lit} shows
the consequence: the same architecture that improves one kind of task by a large margin makes another kind
worse~\cite{kim2025scaling}, and many failures can be traced to system design and coordination and not only
to the underlying model~\cite{cemri2025mast}.

We state the problem as follows. A task is decomposed into a dependency graph $G=(V,E)$ of subtasks. A pool
$\mathcal{M}=\{m_1,\dots,m_K\}$ of coordination mechanisms is available behind one interface, together with
a direct route that answers a subtask with a single model call. For each subtask $v$ that becomes ready, the
coordinator chooses $\pi(v)\in\mathcal{M}\cup\{\text{direct}\}$ from features it can observe before
execution and from the outcomes it has recorded so far. It never sees the reference answer of a task, only
the runtime checks described in Section~\ref{sec:eval}. The aim is to maximize the probability that the
final answer is correct while the total cost, counted in tokens and model calls and including the
coordinator's own share, stays within a budget $B$. A wrong choice is paid for twice, once in wasted budget
and once in the reallocation that follows, so routing errors and communication overhead are part of the
problem and not side effects.

The project sets out to measure three things: whether mechanisms that share one backbone model differ in
what they are good at; if they do, whether a selector can exploit the difference using cheap features; and
whether the biological mechanisms add anything over well-designed conventional agents once compute is held
equal.

\subsection{Objectives and research questions}

\begin{enumerate}[label=O\arabic*.]
  \item Build a modular coordinator with a common interface for heterogeneous mechanisms, starting with ant,
        bee and octopus and designed to take all seven proposed modules.
  \item Measure the strengths of each mechanism across task families and build capability profiles from
        correctness, cost and latency.
  \item Design allocation policies, first rule-based and then learned from recorded outcomes, and test
        them within a fixed budget.
  \item Separate the effect of the mechanisms from the effect of extra computation, using matched
        baselines and component ablations.
  \item Test recovery from injected failures and find the point at which coordination stops paying for
        itself.
\end{enumerate}

The objectives correspond to the five research questions of the approved synopsis. \textbf{RQ1:} Do the
mechanisms have complementary strengths? \textbf{RQ2:} Can dynamic allocation improve performance within a
fixed budget? \textbf{RQ3:} What value do the biological mechanisms add? \textbf{RQ4:} Can the framework
adapt to failures and changing task requirements? \textbf{RQ5:} When does coordination become inefficient?

\section{Brief Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit}

\runin{Fixed workflows and their cost.}
Most LLM multi-agent frameworks fix the roles and the communication pattern when the system is designed.
He, Treude and Lo~\cite{he2025lma} review 71 primary studies of such systems in software engineering and
place the optimization of agent collaboration as the second phase of their research agenda, after the
capabilities of individual agents. The cost of fixed designs is now documented. Cemri et
al.~\cite{cemri2025mast} built a taxonomy of 14 failure modes in three groups (system design, inter-agent
misalignment and task verification) from expert analysis of 150 execution traces, and used it to annotate
more than 1,600 traces from seven popular frameworks. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2024rethinking} found that a
single agent with a strong prompt comes close to the best multi-agent discussion method on a range of
reasoning benchmarks. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2025agentprune} treated redundant communication as a problem
in its own right and showed that many messages can be pruned. In a controlled study, Kim et
al.~\cite{kim2025scaling} compared a single agent with four multi-agent architectures on six benchmarks and
three model families under standardized tools, prompts and compute. The relative change in performance
against the single agent ranged from $+80.8\%$ on a decomposable financial reasoning task to $-70.0\%$ on
sequential planning. Their conclusion, that coordination helps only when it matches the structure of the
task, is the starting point of this project.

\runin{Adaptive topology and routing.}
A second line of work lets the structure depend on the task. GPTSwarm~\cite{zhuge2024gptswarm} describes
agents as computational graphs and optimizes both the prompts at the nodes and the connections between
them. DyLAN~\cite{liu2024dylan} selects a team for a task using an agent importance score.
G-Designer~\cite{zhang2025gdesigner} generates a communication topology for each task with a variational
graph auto-encoder, and AFlow~\cite{zhang2025aflow} searches over workflows written as code using Monte
Carlo tree search. RouteLLM~\cite{ong2025routellm} learns to send each query to a stronger or a weaker
model. MasRouter~\cite{yue2025masrouter} brings these decisions together: a cascaded controller chooses the
collaboration mode, the agent roles and the LLM for each role, and reports up to 52\% lower overhead than
earlier methods on HumanEval. Work from 2026 routes at a finer grain. GraphPlanner~\cite{feng2026graphplanner}
chooses a model and an agent role at every step of a workflow, using a graph memory of past interactions.
AdaptOrch~\cite{yu2026adaptorch} picks one of four topologies from the dependency graph of the task.
Symphony-Coord~\cite{guan2026symphony} is the closest to our coordinator. It routes each subtask to an
executor agent with a LinUCB bandit over task and agent features, updates the bandit from execution
feedback, and reports recovery under agent failures and distribution shift. Adaptive routing of subtasks
with feedback is therefore established. In all of these systems the candidates being selected are agents,
roles, models or graph shapes.

\runin{Bio-inspired coordination with LLMs.}
Wu et al.~\cite{wu2025ecllm} survey the two-way relationship between LLMs and evolutionary computation,
including LLMs that generate and improve optimization algorithms. Two recent preprints apply swarm ideas to
agent coordination directly, and both draw on ant colonies. AMRO-S~\cite{wang2026amros} models routing as
the selection of a path through layers of agents and applies ant colony optimization, with separate
pheromone memories for different task types and a small fine-tuned model that infers the intent of a
query; its ablations concern the router. SwarmSys~\cite{li2025swarmsys} cycles explorer, worker and
validator agents and uses a pheromone-inspired reinforcement rule for task allocation; its ablations remove
the matching and the roles. There is also a skeptical reading. Rahman et al.~\cite{rahman2026swarms}
re-implemented Boids and ant colony optimization with LLM agents and found that the LLM version of Boids
needed about 300 times the computation time of the classical one. Outside the LLM setting,
Dama\v{s}evi\v{c}ius and Maskeli\={u}nas~\cite{damasevicius2021} combine the bat and krill herd algorithms
by switching agents between them according to performance, an early form of the selection studied here.
Camacho-Villal\'on et al.~\cite{camacho2023} analyzed six highly cited animal-named optimizers component by
component and argued that their metaphors hide ideas that already existed under other names. We take this
as a design rule: every module in this project is specified by what it computes, and each is tested against
plain counterparts that lack its distinguishing components.

\runin{Biological basis and software counterparts.}
Seeley et al.~\cite{seeley2012} showed that scout bees committed to one nest site deliver stop signals to
scouts advertising a rival site, and that this cross inhibition lets a swarm settle on one option even when
two are equally good. Its nearest software relative is majority voting over independent
samples~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}, which has no inhibition and no quorum. Sumbre et
al.~\cite{sumbre2001} showed that the arm extension of the octopus is produced by a motor program in the
arm itself, with the brain issuing the command but not the details, a division that resembles a coordinator
handing a bounded job to a worker. Taylor et al.~\cite{taylor2007} observed New Caledonian crows using a
short tool to obtain a longer one that could reach food. In LLM systems the corresponding ideas are tool
making and reuse~\cite{cai2024latm}, planning of function calls as a dependency graph~\cite{kim2024llmcompiler},
and tool learning more generally~\cite{qin2025tool}. Kano et al.~\cite{kano2017} built a brittle-star-like
robot that adapts immediately to the loss of arms through decentralized control. For LLM agents, Huang et
al.~\cite{huang2025resilience} injected agents that make mistakes into six systems and found that
hierarchical structures lost the least performance (5.5\%), while Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2025whowhen}
showed that even the best automatic method names the agent responsible for a failure only 53.5\% of the
time. Theraulaz et al.~\cite{theraulaz1998} modeled division of labor in insect colonies by letting each
completed task lower an individual's response threshold for that task, so that specialists emerge without
central assignment; a selector that reinforces the choices that worked is a loose analogue. Experience
stores for LLM agents~\cite{zhao2024expel,zhang2025memory} are the related software mechanism behind the
proposed elephant module.

\runin{What this project adds.}
Adaptive routing, selection from feedback and recovery all exist in the work above, and we do not claim to
introduce them. The open question is narrower. Table~\ref{tab:related} lists what the closest systems
select: agents, roles, models and topologies. We are not aware of a study that implements several
coordination mechanisms with different control flow, memory and stopping rules behind one interface, runs
them on the same subtasks at the same budget, and reports which mechanism suits which kind of subtask and
which component of each is responsible. This project is an empirical study of that question. Its selector
deliberately reuses a known method, the LinUCB routing of~\cite{guan2026symphony}, so that a difference in
results comes from what is selected and not from how the selection is made.

\section{Proposed Methodology}

\subsection{Architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the planned system. The coordinator decomposes the incoming task into a
dependency graph, selects an executor for each subtask that is ready, dispatches it with a share of the
budget and applies the runtime checks of Section~\ref{sec:eval} to the result. A result that passes is
aggregated into the answer. One that fails is reallocated, which is a new selection made with the failed
attempt on record. Every executor implements the same call: it takes a subtask, its context and a budget,
and returns the output, a confidence estimate, the cost (prompt and completion tokens, model calls and
elapsed time), a status flag and the full trace.

The decomposer, the runtime checks, the tools, the backbone model and the prompt templates for shared steps
are common to every system we compare, including the baselines. Executors differ in control flow,
communication rules, memory updates and stopping conditions. Sharing the rest removes the obvious
confounds, and the ablations of Section~\ref{sec:eval} are what tie an effect to a particular component.

\subsection{Mechanisms}
\label{sec:mech}

The modules are of two kinds (Table~\ref{tab:mech}). An executor solves a subtask and is something the
coordinator can select. A wrapper cannot solve a subtask by itself; it adds recovery or memory to whichever
executor is running. Direct, ant, bee and octopus form the core pool and are built first. The starfish
wrapper follows, since the fault study needs it. Crow is added afterwards as an extended pool, and wolf and
elephant are attempted only if the core study is on schedule. The starfish module is named loosely: its
model is the brittle-star robot of~\cite{kano2017}, and brittle stars and starfish are distinct groups.

\runin{Ant.}
The executor builds a solution as a sequence of choices, such as which tool to call or which sub-goal to
try next. Choices come from a fixed vocabulary for the task family, so that what is learned on one path
applies to the next. At each step one model call proposes and ranks the candidates, and the rank gives a
prior $\eta_c$. A weight $\tau_{f,c}$ is kept for each task feature $f$ and choice $c$, starting from
$\tau_0$, and a choice is sampled with probability proportional to $\tau_{f,c}^{\alpha}\,\eta_c^{\beta}$.
When a finished path has been scored by the runtime checks with reward $r\in[0,1]$, every weight under $f$
evaporates and the choices on the path receive a deposit:
\[
  \tau_{f,c}\leftarrow\max\bigl(\tau_{\min},\,(1-\rho)\,\tau_{f,c}\bigr)\ \text{for all } c,
  \qquad
  \tau_{f,c}\leftarrow\tau_{f,c}+\delta\,r\ \text{for } c \text{ on the path.}
\]
The three controls follow directly. With $\delta=0$ nothing is reinforced and sampling follows the prior.
With $\rho=0$ nothing is forgotten. With $\alpha=\beta=0$ the sampler is uniform.

\runin{Bee.}
The module runs $k$ scouts. Each starts uncommitted and answers the subtask independently, and scouts whose
normalized answers match support the same option. In each later round an uncommitted scout is shown the
argument of a committed scout, drawn in proportion to the current support of each option, and commits to
that option if it accepts the argument. This is recruitment. A committed scout that receives a stop signal
from a supporter of a rival option returns to the uncommitted state with probability $\sigma$. This is
cross inhibition. The module answers as soon as one option is held by a quorum of $\theta k$ scouts. If the
budget runs out first it returns the option with the most support, and breaks ties by summed confidence.
The procedure is our adaptation of the process described in~\cite{seeley2012} and not a fit of their model.
Its controls are the same procedure with $\sigma=0$, and a majority vote over the same number of model
calls. All parameters of both modules are tuned on development tasks only.

\subsection{Allocation policy}

The arms of the selector are the executors, including the direct route. Wrappers are not arms. Starfish is
switched on or off for a whole experiment, and elephant is evaluated as a component that is present or
absent. The coordinator describes each ready subtask by features that are available before execution: the
number of candidate options, whether a runtime check exists for the result, the tools it needs and the
depth of their dependencies, the number of sibling subtasks that are ready at the same time, an estimate of
its length, and the recorded outcomes of similar subtasks.

Allocation is developed in two stages. The first is a small rule table derived from the executor profiles
of RQ1. The second is a contextual bandit over the same features, LinUCB~\cite{li2010linucb}, as used for
subtask routing in~\cite{guan2026symphony}. The reward of a choice is $s-\lambda c$, where $c$ is the
normalized token cost and $\lambda$ sets the trade-off. The success term $s$ has two sources. The runtime
check of the subtask gives a signal that would be available in any deployment. On the training split each
choice is also credited with the graded outcome of its parent task. That label exists for training only and
is never shown to the coordinator on test tasks.

Two protocols keep learning and evaluation apart. In the \emph{frozen} protocol the selector, the ant
weights and the episode store are fixed after development, nothing is carried from one test task to the
next, and all state is reset between seeds and between systems. It is used for RQ1, RQ2, RQ3 and RQ5. In
the \emph{online} protocol, used for RQ4, tasks arrive as an ordered stream with a controlled change in the
task mix or the fault rate, and the selector keeps updating from runtime checks alone. It is compared with
its own frozen copy on the same stream.

\subsection{Evaluation plan}
\label{sec:eval}

\runin{What the systems see.}
Reference answers stay inside the evaluation harness. They are used to grade the final answer of a task
and, on the training split, to produce training labels. During a run a system sees only runtime checks:
the status returned by a tool, the schema and type of an output, constraint checkers that are a public
part of the task, tests supplied with the task, and agreement between independent samples. For a planning
task the plan validator is a legitimate runtime check, because the domain model is part of the input. For
a question-answering subtask no such check exists, and the executor has to rely on tool status, format and
agreement. A subtask without a reliable check passes with its confidence estimate, and the selector learns
from that weaker evidence. Every system, baselines included, gets the same checks and the same allowance of
retries.

\runin{Tasks.}
Three families of 150 tasks each are planned. Planning uses Blocksworld and Logistics instances from
PlanBench with its plan validator~\cite{valmeekam2023planbench} and validation queries from TravelPlanner
with its official evaluator~\cite{xie2024travelplanner}. Tool use uses GAIA validation questions of levels
1 and 2 that can be answered with text and file tools~\cite{mialon2024gaia}, graded by normalized exact
match, together with a small suite of our own built on deterministic local tools. Reasoning uses GSM8K and
MATH, as in~\cite{yue2025masrouter}. Our models and subsets differ from the published ones, so the numbers
will allow only a rough comparison. We will also generate synthetic tasks in which the number of
alternatives, the dependency depth, the parallel width and the fault rate are set by us, so that the
hypotheses of RQ1 can be tested one factor at a time. The 450 tasks are split at the level of the task or
task template, before any subtasks exist, into training (150), tuning (90) and held-out test (210) sets
with equal shares of each family. One task variant per family appears only in the test set.

\runin{Systems compared.}
(i) A single agent with the same tools and retries. (ii) Each core executor used alone. The best of them
is chosen on the tuning split; the best in hindsight on the test set is reported only as an upper bound.
(iii) Fixed allocation, with one executor assigned to each task family by hand. (iv) Random allocation.
(v) A conventional coordinator with specialist agents: a planner that reads the task graph, an executor
role that works each subtask in an act--observe loop, and a critic that reviews each result and may ask for
one revision. It has the same decomposer, checks, tools, token cap and retries, and its prompts are tuned
on the tuning split with the same number of tuning runs as ours. (vi) A conventional adaptive system: the
same LinUCB selector choosing among ordinary workers (direct call, act--observe loop, majority vote,
plan-then-execute). This baseline has adaptivity without the biological mechanisms and is the direct test
of RQ3. (vii) Adaptive allocation over the core pool, rule-based and learned. Where its released code runs
on our backbone, MasRouter~\cite{yue2025masrouter} will be added on the reasoning family as an outside
reference; it is not part of the core comparison.

\runin{Primary comparison and statistics.}
One comparison is fixed in advance as primary: learned adaptive allocation against the best single executor
chosen on the tuning split, on the held-out test set, at a token cap of twice the mean cost of the single
agent, with task success as the measure. The cost claim uses a non-inferiority margin of five percentage
points, so lower cost counts only if success is not worse by more than that. Each configuration is run
with three seeds, and each run is a fresh sample. Repeated runs of one task are not independent tasks, so
uncertainty is estimated with a paired bootstrap that resamples tasks and averages over seeds within a
task. All other comparisons (the remaining baselines, single families, other budgets, ablations) are
secondary, are reported with Holm correction and are labeled exploratory. When an interval includes zero we
will report that no difference was detected and will not read it as equivalence.

\runin{Measurements.}
Task success; tokens divided into coordinator, worker and inter-agent communication; model calls; elapsed
time on uncached runs; and successes per million tokens. Success is also reported at half and at twice the
primary token cap for four systems, since a single operating point can favor any method. Routing regret is
measured on a replay set of about 200 subtask contexts saved from development runs, each executed by every
core executor from the same state; the regret of a choice is the reward of the best executor on that
context minus the reward of the chosen one.

\runin{Faults and recovery.}
Three faults are injected at fixed rates: a tool call that fails once, a worker that times out for the rest
of the task, and a corrupted intermediate result. The third follows the error injection
of~\cite{huang2025resilience}; the first two are our additions. The recovery rate is the share of
fault-affected tasks that still end with a correct answer. Starfish is compared with plain retry and with
restarting the subtask. A sample of failed runs will be labeled with the taxonomy of~\cite{cemri2025mast}.

\runin{Threats to validity.}
Hosted models change without notice, so model versions will be pinned and the primary comparison repeated
on an open-weight model with fixed weights. A response cache is used for debugging and exact replay only.
It is switched off for measured runs, and latency is taken from uncached runs. Public benchmarks may have
been seen in training, which is one reason for the synthetic and in-house tasks. With 210 test tasks the
study can detect only fairly large effects, roughly eight percentage points or more in success, and the
report will state this limit next to every claim.

\runin{Compute.}
No model is trained; the cost is inference. Table~\ref{tab:compute} lists every experiment in the plan and

hosted models at the time of writing. That gives US\$26 to US\$50, or up to about US\$65 with a 30\%

GPU time is not guaranteed, so this replication is the first item to shrink. If money or quota falls
short, the minimum study is the profiling, the main comparison and the executor ablations, about 11,800

\section{Expected Outcomes}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A modular research prototype.} A coordinator with a common interface for heterogeneous
        bio-inspired agents, validated first with three mechanisms and able to take all seven.
  \item \textbf{Capability profiles.} Evidence of which mechanism suits which class of subtask, and of
        when combining them is useful.
  \item \textbf{Comparative results.} Task success, token use, inference cost, completion time and recovery
        after controlled failures, for the adaptive system and for every baseline at equal budget.
  \item \textbf{Component-level analysis.} Ablations that separate the effects of adaptive routing, memory,
        communication and the agent-specific mechanisms.
  \item \textbf{Reproducible materials.} Code, configurations, evaluation scripts and experiment logs in a
        public repository, supporting the final report and a possible conference submission.
\end{itemize}

The working hypothesis is that adaptive allocation improves task success at an equal budget, or lowers cost
at comparable success. It is tested by the primary comparison of Section~\ref{sec:eval}. If the
conventional adaptive system or the specialist coordinator matches the bio-inspired system within the
uncertainty of the experiment, the report will state that no additional benefit of the biological
mechanisms was detected. Cases in which a single agent is the better choice are the answer to RQ5. As in
the synopsis, the project maps to SDG~9 (target 9.5) as primary and SDG~8 (target 8.2) as secondary, and no
energy or carbon claim will be drawn from token savings.

\section{Timeline and Work Plan}

The plan covers the seventh and eighth semesters, from October 2026 to May 2027. Each column of
Figure~\ref{fig:gantt} is half a month. Evaluation dates are indicative and will follow the department
